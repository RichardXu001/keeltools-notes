Note 08 · Discovery

Four paths to discovering new AI tools: from RSS to directories

2026-10-23 · ~6 min

AI tool launches outrun anyone's manual tracking. What scales isn't following more accounts — it's splitting discovery into four fixed channels, each with its own open frequency.

The short version: news sources answer “what shipped”, community answers “who actually uses it”, directories answer “what are all the options”, roundups answer “is switching worth it”. Four jobs, four channels — miss one and you have a blind spot.

01 The four channels

Four channels, four questions
ChannelAnswersCadence
News and release feeds (RSS, changelogs)What shipped this weekOnce a week, batched
Community (HN, Reddit, X)Who really uses it, where it breaksAs it comes; big news finds you
Tool directories (KeelTools, VaneTools)What the whole category looks likeDuring active evaluation
Roundups and comparisonsWho's worth switching toQuarterly

The directory channel gets underrated: it answers the “full set” question, while the other three only hand you increments. Wondering how many schools of meeting-note tools exist? Ten community threads beat no single answer; one pass through a categorized directory beats all ten. The KeelTools directory splits by tool / skill / prompt; VaneTools' category pages carry 38,000+ resources. Different granularity, deliberately complementary.

02 Community: watch “used”, not “launched”

Launch posts are written by vendors; usage posts are written by users. The tell is simple: does the post contain a concrete task, a concrete failure, a concrete price? A recommendation without failure detail is an ad by default. Hacker News threads on “which AI tools are actually useful” are a different shape — top replies tend to come from people running the tool in production, an order of magnitude more credible than launch posts.

03 News: follow changelogs, not headlines

A tool's official changelog is the most underrated source: pricing changes, quota adjustments and model swaps land there first, days before secondhand coverage. An RSS aggregator keeps a pile of changelogs manageable — and the selection logic in the KeelTools blog's tool-choice piece applies: list the need first, pick the form second, don't install ten apps to chase news.

04 Roundups: read the method, not the rank

A roundup's value is its dimensions, not its winner. A list that shows ranks without method is useless to you — if the dimensions don't match your scenario, the ranking means nothing. Check three things: sample size, pricing basis, refresh cadence. Data-driven breakdowns like VaneTools' technical blog read far better than “top ten of the year” lists.

05 Make it a table

Put the four channels on a weekly grid: ten minutes for RSS on Monday, community whenever, directories during evaluation, roundups at quarter end. Total time stays under half an hour a week — once a discovery channel costs an hour, it stops being a tool and becomes a chore, you stop feeding it, and you drift back to algorithmic feeds.

If you keep one rule: directories hold the full set, community holds the truth, changelogs hold the deltas, roundups hold the switch decision — four channels, four questions; never make one do four jobs.
